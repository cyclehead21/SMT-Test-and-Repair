## 1. Overview:

The Hydraulic Pressure Unit (HPU) is a hydraulic pump assembly that provides power for the Toyota SMT hydraulic system. The HPU is bolted to the rear suspension crossmember, below the air filter box on the left side of the car. The assembly contains an electric motor - a fixed angle swash-plate pump with five tiny pistons, a hydraulic accumulator, fluid reservoir and two solenoids. Part number 31360-0W010.

You can remove and reinstall the HPU without performing any “relearn” processes, since it only provides hydraulic power to the Gear Selector Assembly (GSA).

The common failure in the HPU is for the seal on the electric motor shaft to wear out and let fluid leak into the motor, which can ruin the motor. The only other critical seals in the HPU are the o-rings on the two solenoids. If they get worn they can allow pressure to leak between chambers and cause trouble. The rest of the seals in the HPU are just “static” seals and o-rings that prevent fluid leaks.

Example of solenoid o-ring failure: I had an o-ring on my clutch control solenoid start to leak. It made the car lurch and jerk during start off, so I looked like a teenager learning to drive a clutch. This behavior got worse as the system heated up. Pressure was leaking between two chambers on the solenoid (past an o-ring) The system did not throw any error codes. Solution: replace solenoid orings.

### SMT Fluid:

Ref Toyota SMT Fluid P/N: 08886-02206

SMT fluid choice has been debated for years. Toyota sells “SMT Fluid”. It is very expensive.

I recommend straight DOT3/4 brake fluid.

Extensive discussion of the thinking and reasons behind this are here: [SMT Fluid Discussion](smt-fluid-discussion.md)

### Repair Logic

#### Leaks:

If the HPU is not leaking fluid from the bottom of the electric motor or the pump, then likely the shaft seal is fine. The only other reason to tear into the HPU is to replace the solenoid o-rings. If you’re rebuilding the GSA, you could postpone tearing into the HPU until it’s really necessary since removal of the HPU only takes about 15 minutes and doesn’t require a re-learn sequence.

#### Motor:

If the electric motor won’t run, life is tough. I am still hunting for a shop that can replace the commutator on the electric motor. Currently, I don’t know anybody who can repair the HPU electric motor. I’d love a referral! It is possible that “Mamco” would sell a new motor that will fit the HPU. Mamco provides the electric motors that power an identical (Parker design) 5-piston fixed-angle swash plate pump, used in a Carlisle hydro-electric trailer braking system. The Mamco motors have the correct flat tip on the motor shaft, and the same four-bolt pattern for mounting. I have not contacted them (yet) about providing spare electric motors.

#### Accumulator:

The hydraulic accumulator is the black steel sphere screwed into the top of the HPU. It uses a very thin 22mm wrench, or just remove it with a strap wrench around the steel ball. (The ball is very thick steel, you cannot hurt it!)

The accumulator has a rubber bladder inside with pressurized nitrogen on one side, and SMT fluid on the other side. If the accumulator has lost its pre-charge pressure, the pump will run very frequently, and with very short duration. The car may not shift well, especially into Reverse gear. (I think reverse gear requires the most fluid volume to accomplish) The accumulator is easy to check. Remove it from the HPU. Drop a thin rod like a drill-bit into the pressure port. It should go in about an inch or so, until it touches the top of the bladder. (There is a tough blue plastic disc that protects the rubber bladder.) If the rod goes way into the sphere, like 2 inches or more, then the bladder is deflated. It may be possible to have a partially discharged accumulator, or a slowly leaking one. You would need a method of testing the accumulator volume capacity (at full system pressure) to detect this.

This Porsche accumulator will fit but recent tests show it’s <u>not the correct</u> nitrogen pressure:

Porsche 911 6 Cyl 3.8 liter

1995-2012

Turbo Clutch Hydraulic Accumulator

Porsche PN 997-314-166-00

Corteco PN 80000659

### General cleanliness and workmanship

The swash plate pump has some highly polished surfaces, and high rpm pistons. You need to be clean when you’re working with this thing. Grit and crud will ruin the little pump parts. (Useless info: The tightest flatness spec I’ve ever seen on an aircraft component was for a Vought A-7 aircraft hydraulic pump swash plate slipper surface. It was 7 Angstroms! That’s 7 x 10 to the minus 10 meters! )

Obviously the moving hydraulic parts cannot tolerate scratches or gouges. Be careful using tools.

## 2. Removal Summary:

### 2.1 Pressurized fluid caution

Let the car sit overnight with the battery disconnected. This will allow the accumulator pressure to bleed down. No worries with the hydraulic quick disconnects because they have internal seals that prevent fluid squirting out. However cracking open a freshly charged HPU will get you a bath in brake fluid, and could send a jet of pressurized brake fluid into your eyes. I opened a pressurized HPU one time and it hosed down my whole workbench. It’s best to unthread things slowly when you first crack open the HPU, and be prepared for pressurized fluid.

### 2.2 Removal Summary

A. Remove the air filter box completely (top and bottom halves).

B. Unbolt three bolts holding HPU to the crossmember

C. Remove three hydraulic lines and one electrical connector

D. Lift the HPU out

## 3. Removal Steps

### 3.1 Unbolt

(Note: If you plan to remove the pressure accumulator, it’s probably easier to unthread it while the HPU is still bolted to the crossmember. It should unthread using a rubber strap wrench. Trying to unthread the accumulator with the HPU loose on a workbench will be frustrating with the whole thing flopping about.)

There is no need to remove any of the thin aluminum heat shields, they can all stay in place.

Round up all of your ⅜ extensions and stick them together to make a 2 foot long extension. This will allow you to remove the three bolts holding the HPU to the crossmember. Reach inside the heat shields with the long extension, and swing your ratchet wrench in the clear space near the engine hood latch.

### 3.2 Block the vent hole

The reservoir vent cap has a circular relief molded into it that will hold an o-ring. Temporarily install an o-ring to block off the air vent hole in the cap, and keep the brake fluid from dribbling all over your crossmember when you flip the HPU upside down. If you have one of my GSA seal kits - use one of the “GSA Solenoid Base” o-rings as a temporary vent cover. Just pick it off carefully after the HPU is removed, so you can use it during your GSA overhaul. Then keep the old oring from the GSA overhaul to use when reinstalling the HPU.

![GSA solenoid base O-ring used as a temporary reservoir vent cover](../images/hpu-removal-and-overhaul/02-temporary-vent-cover-ring.jpg)

### 3.3 Disconnect

Disconnect the big gray electrical connector. A small screwdriver will help press the plastic push tab, and wiggle the connector apart.

### 3.4 Remove Hydraulic connectors

#### 3.4.1

Flip the whole HPU on its side so the three hydraulic lines are facing up (for better access). The three hydraulic lines are “quick disconnect” joints. They connect very quickly. They are more difficult to dis-connect. Each one has a soft teflon plastic ring. You need to press this ring completely inside, into the connector to unlatch the internal fingers.

Video: [https://youtube.com/shorts/oC_OV4RanMM?feature=share](https://youtube.com/shorts/oC_OV4RanMM?feature=share)

Reference: Clutch pressure supply hose is Blue, GSA pressure supply or Master Pressure hose is Gold, and Fluid return hose is Red.

##### Screwdriver

You can use a worn flathead screwdriver that doesn’t have any sharp edges. Use the flat surfaces of the screwdriver to press the white ring upward, inside the collar. Don’t bugger up the soft white plastic ring. If you want to really protect the white ring, you can wrap a small zip tie around just beneath the white ring and zip it up tight. Then press your screwdriver against the zip tie as a sacrificial tool. This prevents buggering up the white ring. Cut the zip tie off when you’re done.

##### Tool

Alternatively, you can buy or make a nice tool like this. (.100 inch thick steel, .656 inch wide slot with a full radius.) It will press the white ring uniformly.

![Tool pressing the hydraulic quick-disconnect release ring](../images/hpu-removal-and-overhaul/03-hydraulic-disconnect-tool.png)

#### 3.4.2

Press the white ring up inside until it’s hidden from view. You can rotate the whole connector for easy access to the entire perimeter of the white ring. Rotate the connector, push the ring up inside, and walk all the way around each connector. Don’t bother tugging the connector apart until all the white ring is pushed inside, all the way around. When the white ring is all pushed inside, the fingers will be unlatched, and the two halves of the connector will be very easy to pull apart. Sometimes it helps to give the connector a little push in the “closed” direction to give the fingers a little free space to release.

#### 3.4.3

The red and blue connectors are made from anodized aluminum. The gold connector is made from steel so it can rust completely shut if it has been in a corrosive environment. If a connector simply won’t open up, you can unscrew the hose from the HPU body. Be very careful with the tiny wrench flats. They will try to round off. Apply a little PB blaster or your favorite penetrating oil, then tap the flats with a small punch and hammer to loosen. Use a small pair of “Knipex” brand pliers. (They clamp the flats without slipping), or (last resort) a very tight-fitting open end wrench may work. Take pains to avoid rounding off the small flats, the fitting is very soft steel. Don’t go crazy with penetrating oil. All of the rubber parts of the SMT system will swell up and fail if they are soaked with hydraulic oil or petroleum products (grease, motor oil etc)

## 4. Disassembly

### 4.1

Clean up the HPU before you start tearing it apart. The bottom of the HPU is usually crudded up with peeling paint and dirt. Don’t let dirt get into the hydraulic pump while you have it all pulled apart. Remove the steel mounting frame from the pump. Blow everything off with compressed air and get everything nice and clean. Unscrew everything and see what’s inside.

#### 4.1.1 Remove the plastic reservoir

The plastic reservoir is held in place by four torx head screws. The torx head is very soft and the teeth may strip. Use a fresh T20 tip with sharp teeth. Before you attempt to unthread the screws, try hitting the tool with a small hammer to help break the threads loose. If they’re rusted in place, you can drill the heads off. Replace them with some M5x0.8 screws.

![Reservoir Torx screw location](../images/hpu-removal-and-overhaul/04-reservoir-torx-screw.png)

Depinning the large gray connector so you can separate the motor, sensors and solenoids.

[https://youtube.com/shorts/hQW5XXqKnQc?is=Yj3SDr6qzCafnIeq](https://youtube.com/shorts/hQW5XXqKnQc?is=Yj3SDr6qzCafnIeq)

### 4.2

There are four long bolts that hold the pump in place. They require a 4mm hex tool. I used an impact gun to chatter them loose. Everything gets rusty from all the brake fluid holding moisture on the steel parts. I think the impact will stand a better chance of breaking the rusty threads loose, than just torquing them out by hand.

### 4.3

The pressure relief valve has a small steel check-ball inside, held by a small spring. The pump pressure port has a plastic “U” cup, and another (different size) spring and check-ball. The larger ball goes into the pump pressure port. The springs are not the same. Don’t mix them up.

### 4.4

It’s best to remove the pump barrel, pistons and swash plate all as one unit, without separating all the individual components.

If you decide to disassemble - be extra careful removing the five tiny pistons. Do not scratch them up with pliers. Mine acted like they were attached to the shiny wobble plate surface, but it was just Van der Waals forces (?) sticking them together. They can require a tug to get them free. It might be a good idea to keep them in sequence so the little pistons get back into their same bores upon re-assembly. I didn’t keep track and just reassembled them randomly. I haven’t noticed any problems, so it’s your choice.

### 4.5

Caution: If you will have the pump disassembled for more than a day, you must submerge all the shiny pump internal parts in brake fluid to keep moisture and air away. I failed to do this and my shiny parts all showed pitting corrosion after only one week!

### 4.6

Electric motor failure: I think the most common reason for motor failure is the commutator wearing through on the armature. The brushes seem to outlast the commutators. I have disassembled one motor. It is not easy to open, I had to cut the end cap off with a zizz wheel. I’m sure a good auto-electric shop could overhaul the motor, but I can’t find one. I read about a guy that had his motor rebuilt and they installed a new wear surface on the shaft somehow. I’m not sure how that’s done.

### 4.7

While the HPU is all torn apart, it’s a nice time to try to dig any crap out of the reservoir. I have seen lots of brown flaky stuff in reservoirs. I’ve spent time with long q-tips trying to fish it out. A good blast of compressed air may help chase it out also. There also seems to be a filter inside the reservoir so I suspect the flakes all get caught by the filter. I’m not aware of the brown flakes causing trouble in the system.

### 4.8

HPU solenoids are usually difficult to remove. They get rusted into their bores. Soak them with a tiny amount of penetrating oil for a few days. (Don’t worry too much about oil contamination since you’re replacing all the o-rings, but don’t go crazy either.) Remove the two long “set screws” that retain them. The pins require a T25 torx tip. Use a nice sharp tip, you don’t want them to strip. These pins can also be rusted in place. Tap a screwdriver tip between the solenoid and the aluminum HPU housing as a wedge. Work opposite sides to get the solenoid to “walk” out. The base of the solenoid is made from fairly heavy steel so you can be fairly aggressive prying/hammering between the base and HPU body (only). Caution: The canister body of the solenoid is a thin sheet metal cover for the electromagnet, so do not hammer or pry on the thin canister!

If you need to disassemble a solenoid for some reason, here is the procedure. [LINK](solenoids.md)

\- The solenoids have three orings on them. They are successively larger, starting from the tip.

For reference: the upper solenoid is the “master” solenoid and routes pressure to the GSA. The lower (clutch) solenoid only routes pressure to the clutch.

### 4.9 Cleaning

It’s your choice how much you want to disassemble to clean things up. The aluminum housing typically gets a lot of black oxidation and pitting corrosion. Fortunately this is usually limited to the exterior surfaces where it won’t cause much trouble. The one exception is the reservoir base o-ring reliefs. They trap moisture and are usually filled with lots of corrosion.

## 5 Reassembly

### 5.1 Details

#### 5.1.1 Motor and Pump

The electric motor shaft may have a worn ring where the old seal has been riding. I think it’s okay to install the new shaft seal a little deeper in the housing so it will touch a new spot on the shaft. Watch the pump barrel orientation when you reassemble the pump. It has an internal groove whose orientation must be correct. See the picture below.

#### 5.1.2 Solenoids

In my repair seal kit, the two larger o-rings are identical size. **<u>The smallest oring goes on the tip of the solenoid. The two identical sized orings are installed in the middle and largest position.</u>**

Note that the two solenoids are identical in design, but not in function. They can be installed in the wrong position and (obviously) will not work correctly. Photos below show how to check which solenoid goes where. If you do get them installed in the wrong hole, you can de-pin the connector and simply move the wires to the correct position.

### 5.2 Bleeding the pump

#### 5.2.1 Prime

If you reassemble the pump and bolt it back into the car, it will likely run forever without building any pressure. That’s because it has lost its “prime” - there is no fluid to seal the pistons and let them pump. You need to fill the reservoir, and then bleed the pump.

##### 5.2.1.1 Bench Bleed the HPU

I suggest doing the bleeding on the bench. Hook up 12V to the middle of the big gray connector. Look for the fat red and black wires and connect them to +12V and -12V respectively. But first! Remove the pressure relief screw and the spring and the little steel ball. Fill the reservoir. Then connect the pump to 12V to run the motor.

> In-car Bleeding

I believe you can remove the pressure relief screw after the HPU has been bolted back into the car, and bleed it. I’m not positive about this because I’ve never tried. If it’s reachable, I am sure it will be a messy process.

#### 5.2.2 Finger Bleed procedure

As the motor is running, hold your finger over the open pressure relief screw hole (no spring, and no check-ball) and let fluid come out until you feel some pressure. Shut off the motor, keep your finger over the hole to minimize fluid loss. Then screw the check ball, relief valve and cap back into place.

#### 5.3.2 Bench test warning

If you run the pump on the bench, fully assembled, it is possible to over-pressurize the system. I’d say 20 seconds run time is safe if you start with an unpressurized system. Beyond that you need to be monitoring system pressure using the pressure sensor. If you want to do this, look up the pins and Voltage-to-pressure numbers in the big green book (BGB). If you run the pump too long, the relief valve will pop open and make a loud screeching noise.

#### 5.3.3 Pressure

After you finger-bleed the HPU, you want to be sure it’s making pressure. I’d run the pump 20 seconds or so after you screw the relief valve back in place. You should be able to hear the motor straining as the system builds pressure. If not, you can repeat the finger-bleed process. **Caution - protect your eyes.** Be ready for a spray of fluid as you unscrew the relief valve if you’re wrong and the system does indeed have some pressure built up. If you reinstall the HPU into the car and the HPU keeps running longer than 30-40 seconds, it’s probably not primed correctly. You’ll need to remove the battery cables and go through the finger bleeding process again.

You can de-pressurize the system using a techstream “test” function. Or just let the HPU sit overnight, and the pressure will have all bled down.

### 5.4 Vent

Don’t forget to remove the o-ring that you installed to block the vent hole in the reservoir cap!

![Temporary O-ring around the reservoir cap](../images/hpu-removal-and-overhaul/05-reservoir-cap-vent-ring.jpg)

### 5.5 Refill

Refill the reservoir. Note that the accumulator stores a fair amount of fluid. When you check the fluid level in the reservoir, make sure the accumulator has been discharged (by sitting overnight), or you will overfill the reservoir. The fill line max/min marks are indented into the plastic reservoir, just above the plastic seam. If you overfill the reservoir, the excess fluid will get forced out of the vent hole in the cap, and will appear on the ground under your car. If so, just wash it off. No need to check the level again, it will just squirt out any excess until it’s correct.

Explanation of fluid flow in the pump cylinder-head block:

There is a light spring, check ball and plastic cap in the pressure port that prevents fluid reversion. There is a heavy spring and check ball in the pressure relief path. It appears to crack open at 1000psi (roughly 3.55V from the HPU pressure sensor). The pressure relief valve makes a screeching noise when the valve cracks open.

![Annotated return, pressure, and pressure-relief fluid paths](../images/hpu-removal-and-overhaul/37-pump-fluid-paths.png)
